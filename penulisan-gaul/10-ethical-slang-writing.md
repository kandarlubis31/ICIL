# 📝 10 — The Ethical Slang Writer

> 🔴 Advanced | Prereq: 01, 02, 08 | ~10 min

Casual language is not consequence-free. Slang can include, exclude, stereotype, appropriate, and harm. This course provides the ethical framework for writing casually and responsibly — whether for brands, games, fiction, or social media.

---

## 10.1 The Power Dynamics of Slang

| Who Writes | Potential Harm | Mitigation |
|------------|---------------|------------|
| **Brand using youth slang** | Appropriation, "fellow kids" cringe | Hire Gen Z writers, test with target audience |
| **Jakarta writer using regional slang** | Cultural tourism, stereotyping | Consult native speakers, credit sources |
| **Male writer using female slang** | Misrepresentation | Co-write with women, sensitivity review |
| **Out-group using in-group terms** | Appropriation of marginalized community language | Understand term origin before using |
| **Professional using class-marked slang** | Class tourism, poverty cosplay | Respect the socio-economic origins of street slang |

## 10.2 The Exclusion Problem

Every slang term creates an **in-group** (those who understand) and an **out-group** (those who don't). Consider:

| Slang Density | % of Indonesian Population Who Understand |
|---------------|------------------------------------------|
| 10% (light) | ~80% (most urban Indonesians) |
| 30% (medium Jaksel) | ~50% (urban youth 15-35) |
| 60% (TikTok-heavy) | ~25% (Gen Z on social media) |
| 80% (niche gaming/regional) | ~10% (specific community) |

**Rule**: If your content is for a general audience, keep slang density below 20%. If for a niche community, density can go higher — but acknowledge the exclusion explicitly.

## 10.3 Dangerous Terms to Handle With Care

| Category | Examples | Risk |
|----------|----------|------|
| **Mental health trivialization** | *gila, sarap, autis* as casual insults | Stigmatizes mental illness |
| **Class-based slurs** | Derogatory terms for poverty/occupation | Reinforces class prejudice |
| **Regional stereotyping** | "Medan kasar," "Sunda lemes" generalizations | Reduces diverse cultures to stereotypes |
| **Gender policing** | Terms that shame women's sexual agency | Perpetuates double standards |
| **Vulgarity in public comms** | *anjir, mokondo, bangsat* in brand copy | Alienates, damages brand trust |

## 10.4 The Authenticity Checklist

Before publishing casual/slang content, verify:

```
□ Did someone from the target community review this?
□ Does the slang match the actual era/genre of the speaker?
□ Are we punching down (mocking less powerful groups)?
□ Could this term be offensive in a different region?
□ Is the slang density appropriate for the audience?
□ Are we crediting the origin culture/community?
□ Would this sound natural spoken aloud by a native speaker?
```

## 10.5 When NOT to Use Slang

| Context | Why |
|---------|-----|
| Crisis communications | Trust requires clarity, not personality |
| Legal/terms of service | Ambiguity in slang = legal risk |
| Health/safety warnings | Must be understood by ALL audiences |
| Condolences/grief | Casual register disrespects the gravity |
| Accessibility-critical content | Slang creates barriers for non-native speakers |

## 10.6 The Ethical Slang Writer's Manifesto

1. **Slang is borrowed culture, not invented language.** Credit the communities that created it.
2. **Inclusion over coolness.** A broader audience understanding beats an insider wink.
3. **Era awareness.** Using 2020 slang in 2026 content = lazy, not authentic.
4. **No punching down.** Never use slang to mock or belittle marginalized groups.
5. **When in doubt, use plain Indonesian.** Clarity never goes out of style.

## ⚡ Action Checklist

- [ ] Run the Authenticity Checklist before publishing any slang content
- [ ] Calculate your slang density — stay under 20% for general audiences
- [ ] Never use mental health terms as casual insults
- [ ] Credit the communities whose language you're borrowing
- [ ] Know when to go formal: crisis, legal, safety, grief, accessibility
